## Suggested spoken track

### 1. Demand shifts before retailers can respond

Retailers can see what sold. The harder question is what customers wanted but could not buy, and whether the business can respond in time. We propose SAP Retail Signal: one product that connects early demand evidence to a response a planner can explain and carry out.

### 2. SAP already supports the retailer’s core business

SAP already supports retailers’ business operations. Its existing retail customer relationships and applications give this product a route into the systems planners already use.

### 3. Three gaps delay the same decision

Three gaps delay the same decision. Fitting-room interest disappears without a purchase. Online searches for unavailable sizes may never become a restock request. Supplier changes arrive without a clear view of the products and margins they affect.

### 4. Meet Bob, before Retail Signal

Meet Bob, our fictional regional merchandise planner. His sales report looks ordinary, but a cream medium top has only eight units left. Try-on activity and unavailable-size searches sit elsewhere. By the time he checks the supplier’s twenty-one-day lead time, he still has to find stock he can move.

### 5. One product, three connected parts

Play the 90-second recommendation video. It shows three parts of one product, using our working website. The business records are seeded, and SAP and Joule connections are simulated.

### 6. Hardware partners and regional delivery

For fitting-room hardware, we propose Zebra readers and Avery Dennison tags, delivered through regional distributors and local integrators. Ingram Micro would lead the Americas, TD SYNNEX Europe and Asia Pacific, and Redington India, the Middle East and Africa. These partnerships still require commercial and technical validation.

### 7. Meet Bob, with a response he can explain

Now Bob has the evidence and can compare an immediate forty-unit transfer with later replenishment. The transfer scenario raises seven-day coverage from ten to sixty percent, while protecting the source store’s reserve. Thirty-two units remain uncovered. Approval and physical receipt remain separate.

### 8. The first buyers are SAP-connected apparel chains

Our first buyer is an apparel chain with fifty to two hundred fifty stores, online sales and existing SAP systems. Our software market model uses eligible account counts and a reference contract. The counts are assumptions to validate; consumer apparel sales are not our software market.

### 9. Competitors validate the need; integration decides the fit

Competitors already offer fitting-room insight and sophisticated planning. Our proposed advantage is a focused workflow for SAP customers. We should extend existing IBP and CAR capabilities whenever they already solve part of the problem.

### 10. SAP’s ecosystem can shorten the path to action

SAP’s ecosystem can connect evidence to purchasing, inventory and commerce. Authorized business data can improve relevance. Its customer base creates a potential learning advantage, but training rights depend on agreements and consent. We will test accuracy rather than assume more data guarantees it.

### 11. SWOT: a credible extension with execution risk

Our strength is the fit with SAP operations. Our weakness is that integrations and commercial value remain unproven. Strong competitors and constrained retailer budgets make a disciplined pilot essential.

### 12. Dependencies come before automation

The dependencies come first: shared product and location identifiers, reconciled stock, reliable event capture, a secure sandbox and agreed privacy and commercial terms.

### 13. Three risks could stop the pilot

The biggest risks are wrong recommendations, shopper privacy concerns and weak economics. Fresh-stock checks and approval protect decisions. Garment-only collection reduces privacy exposure. A measured pilot gives us a reason to stop if the value is not there.

### 14. Decision rights resolve competing stakeholder needs

Decision rights are explicit. Bob proposes actions; the source store protects its reserve. IT can block invalid data, privacy approves collection, and the CFO owns the scale gate. Partners earn hardware and deployment revenue, with clear support responsibilities.

### 15. Trade-offs are deliberate

We accept trade-offs. Human approval adds time. A narrow apparel launch limits reach. Selective RFID reduces coverage but avoids a full hardware rollout before value is proven. Live predictive news analysis remains future work.

### 16. A 12-week pilot earns the right to scale

The twelve-week pilot starts with baseline interviews and stock mapping. Digital and hardware work then proceed in parallel. Recommendations run in shadow mode before controlled actions. The final weeks compare outcomes with matched stores and produce a scale-or-stop decision.

### 17. Success means a faster, verified response

Our targets are fifty percent faster response, at least ninety-eight percent stock reconciliation, and seventy percent weekly planner use. The financial gate requires positive incremental contribution and a credible cash payback within twenty-four months.

### 18. One subscription; partners earn the hardware work

The illustrative one-product subscription is three hundred fifty thousand Canadian dollars for a hundred-store customer. Partners invoice hardware separately. The hardware allocation is sixty-two and a half percent equipment, twelve and a half percent distribution and twenty-five percent installation. These are proposed terms.

### 19. Retailer value must clear the cash-only test

The retailer case separates cash from time freed. At one percent recovered sales, contribution is four hundred fifty thousand dollars. Capacity freed adds economic value but may not save cash. Economic payback is six months; cash-only payback is twenty-eight. The base case misses our gate, so we need better measured benefits or lower costs before scaling.

### 20. SAP’s base scenario reaches CAD 38.1M in year 3

For SAP, the base adoption scenario reaches thirty-eight point one million dollars of year-three revenue and fifty-two point five million of ending recurring revenue. This assumes midyear wins and no churn. The appendix shows formulas and downside scenarios; these are projections, not SAP guidance.

### 21. Earlier evidence is useful only when teams can act

Bob gets an explainable decision. Shoppers get a better chance of finding their size. The retailer can test whether earlier evidence improves availability and margin.

### 22. A pilot decision, with a clear scale gate

We ask for a capped two hundred fifty thousand dollar pilot, one apparel design partner and sandbox access. Scale only when the response is faster, stock is trustworthy and cash economics pass. SAP Retail Signal makes an early signal useful by connecting it to an accountable response.

## What the website does

The website is the prototype of the proposed product. It is not an installed SAP product, and it does not authenticate to a customer SAP tenant. Seeded business records make the interactions demonstrable without enterprise access.

| Tab | Simple purpose | How it supports the product |
|---|---|---|
| Command Center | A daily overview of demand exceptions and the recommended next step | Brings the three parts into one starting point |
| Opportunities | A prioritized worklist of product/location decisions | Opens evidence and lets Bob compare response options |
| Demand Intelligence | In-store and online interest alongside purchases | Covers smart fitting-room signals and the out-of-stock tracker |
| Location Intelligence | A map of locations, gaps and stock-transfer options | Helps planners find feasible nearby stock |
| Risk & Seasonality | Demand assumptions, timing and no-action exposure | Helps compare what happens if the business waits |
| Inventory & Supply | Inventory, supplier relationships, materials and shortage simulations | Provides the supply-chain risk and response view |
| SAP Workflow | Proposal, approval, goods issue and goods receipt | Demonstrates the sequence that a live S/4HANA integration would follow |
| Action Center | Decisions, owners and follow-up records | Keeps proposed actions accountable |
| Integrations | The roles and status of proposed data/application connections | Distinguishes simulated handoffs from production integration |
| About | Product scope and demo limitations | Explains the competition concept |

Selecting a material in Inventory & Supply shows its description, unit of purchase, prominent suppliers, illustrative purchase rates and public benchmark context. The shortage controls independently set input availability, price, delay and supplier cost pass-through. A graph traces the affected entities. The cost model adds only the price uplift, since baseline input costs are already included in finished-garment costs.

The assistant is **deterministic**: its responses follow programmed calculations and rules. It does not call an external large language model or live SAP Joule. This is useful for demonstrating a reproducible decision, but production AI requires grounding, access controls and evaluation.

## SAP integration in plain language

| Product / term | Meaning | Proposed role |
|---|---|---|
| SAP S/4HANA / ERP | Enterprise resource planning: the business’s records and operational processes | Purchasing and stock transport transactions |
| SAP CAR | Customer Activity Repository: a common view of retail transactions and inventory context | Sales and stock inputs |
| SAP Commerce Cloud | SAP’s digital commerce application | Online events and updated sellable availability |
| SAP IBP | Integrated Business Planning: demand, supply and inventory planning | Reuse planning services and accept approved planning handoffs |
| SAP BTP | Business Technology Platform: services for extending business applications | Host the extension and its controlled connections |
| Integration Suite | Services that connect applications and route data | Secure interfaces, monitoring and reliable event delivery |
| Joule | SAP’s AI assistant | Explain relevant evidence and invoke approved business actions in a future integration |

A production flow would collect garment and online events, validate product/location identifiers, join authorized stock and supplier data, calculate an opportunity, explain the evidence, and let an authorized person approve an action. S/4HANA would remain the operational record. Receipt confirmation would update available inventory; a successful recommendation alone would not.

The production dependencies include a server-side business database, tenant isolation, user authentication, permissions, audit storage, interface mappings, monitoring, and protection against duplicate requests. None of those should be inferred from the demo’s SAP-like visual styling.

## Technical terms the team should know

- **SKU:** Stock-keeping unit; a particular sellable variant, such as a cream medium top.
- **RFID:** Radio-frequency identification. A reader detects a garment’s tag using radio signals. Calibration prevents nearby racks from being counted as fitting-room activity.
- **POS:** Point of sale, the checkout system.
- **Conversion:** Purchases divided by qualified interactions. The denominator matters; a try-on is not an identified unique shopper.
- **Deduplication:** Removing repeated records so one session/SKU pair does not count multiple times.
- **Bot:** Automated traffic. Production classification should distinguish low-quality traffic from relevant shopping agents.
- **API:** A controlled interface through which one application requests data or an action from another.
- **Authentication / authorization:** Proving who a user or service is / deciding what it may do.
- **Tenant:** A customer’s separate application and data environment.
- **Grounding:** Providing relevant authorized business evidence to an AI response.
- **Training:** Changing an AI model using examples. Grounding a response does not automatically grant training rights.
- **Rule-based / deterministic:** The same inputs produce the same programmed explanation or calculation.
- **localStorage:** Data saved in the browser. It is appropriate for this demo, not a production shared inventory database.
- **React / TypeScript / Vite:** The interface library, typed programming language, and development/build tooling used by the prototype.
- **UI5 / Fiori:** SAP’s interface components and design conventions. Visual consistency supports familiar interaction; it is not proof of a live SAP connection.
- **Goods issue / goods receipt:** Recording dispatch from the source / arrival at the destination.
- **Protected reserve:** Stock retained at the source location instead of offering every unit for transfer.
- **Lead time:** Time before supply is expected to arrive. The demo’s supplier lead time already includes production; do not add production again.
- **MMBtu:** One million British thermal units, a measure of energy.
- **Pass-through:** The share of an added supplier input cost charged to the retailer. The demo keeps retail prices fixed.
- **Percentage points:** The difference between percentages. A margin moving from 68.20% to 67.89% falls 0.31 percentage points.
- **Contribution margin:** Revenue remaining after the variable costs of earning that revenue. The retailer ROI assumes 45% incremental contribution after variable fulfillment costs and returns.
- **TAM / SAM / SOM:** Total potential software spend / initially serviceable spend / the portion the business aims to obtain. Our account counts are hypotheses, not a census.
- **ARR:** Annual recurring revenue at a point in time; distinct from revenue earned during the year.
- **COGS:** Cost of goods sold. In the SAP model, variable hosting and support are assumed to be 25% of subscription revenue.
- **ROI / payback:** Value relative to cost / time needed for net benefits to recover upfront cost. Cash benefits and capacity value must be separated.
- **KPI:** Key performance indicator: a defined measurement of success.
- **Shadow mode:** Calculate recommendations without executing them.
- **Matched-store comparison:** Compare pilot stores with similar stores and pre-pilot outcomes to reduce bias. A small pilot may still lack enough data to prove an effect.

## Financial points to defend

All financial projections, prices, partner splits and benefits are illustrative assumptions created for this proposal. The Launchpad does not provide actual willingness-to-pay, deployment quotes or measured customer benefits.

1. A 100-store reference contract is CAD 50,000 platform + 100 × CAD 3,000 = CAD 350,000 per year.
2. Selective RFID deployment equips 20 stores: 20 × CAD 8,000 + CAD 75,000 onboarding = CAD 235,000 upfront. New merchandise-tag programs and incremental retailer infrastructure need separate quotes.
3. Recovered contribution is CAD 100M annual sales × 1% incremental sales × 45% margin = CAD 450,000.
4. Capacity value is 100 stores × 2 hours/week × 52 × CAD 35 = CAD 364,000. It is not automatically cash saved.
5. Net economic annual value is CAD 464,000 after subscription. Cash-only annual value is CAD 100,000. Simple payback is 6.1 versus 28.2 months before ramp-up.
6. The 24-month cash gate needs about 1.039% incremental sales at the same costs/margin, or equivalent price/cost reductions or proven cash savings.
7. SAP revenue uses average active accounts, not ending accounts. Year 3 has 105 average accounts, CAD 36.75M subscription revenue and CAD 1.35M net onboarding fees. Ending ARR is CAD 52.5M.
8. Hardware is partner-invoiced, excluded from modeled SAP revenue. Proposed onboarding revenue is shown net of the delivery partner’s share; finance must confirm the proper accounting treatment.
9. The SAP model is operating contribution before tax, financing, working capital and corporate allocations. Zero churn is optimistic. Downside scenarios are provided.
10. Deferred production in the gas scenario is not necessarily permanently lost sales. Do not add the separate timeline’s revenue exposure to the cost model’s gross-profit reduction.

## Likely Q&A

**Why not use IBP or CAR?** Reuse them. The proposed addition is earlier intent and a simpler evidence-to-action experience. Do not claim a need for duplicate planning software if the existing deployment already solves the workflow.

**What proves a fit problem?** Nothing in try-on conversion alone. Confirm causes through availability checks, returns, fit feedback and controlled investigation.

**Is the AI live?** No. The assistant is a deterministic demo. SAP Joule integration and production model evaluation are future work.

**Are hardware partners signed?** No. Their public product capabilities and regional presence support the proposed shortlist. Specific products, authorization, pricing, supply and installation need qualification.

**Can SAP train on every client’s data?** No. Data use depends on customer agreements, product settings, permissions and applicable consent. The immediate advantage is authorized business context. Cross-customer learning cannot be assumed.

**Why fund a pilot if cash payback fails the gate?** To establish whether measured benefits, negotiated costs or actual cash savings can clear the threshold before a rollout commitment. The base model is a decision tool, not evidence that rollout should already be approved.

**What did market research establish?** A substantial SAP retail footprint and existing competitive solutions. The account-based software TAM remains illustrative and needs an eligible-account list and interviews. The Launchpad’s broad commerce figures provide context, not software revenue.
